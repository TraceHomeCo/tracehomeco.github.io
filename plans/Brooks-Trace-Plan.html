Quick primer
A few terms you'll see below.

These are some of the key chemicals and toxins found in everyday home products and materials — the ones worth recognizing as flags when they show up in what you're already using. Individually, many of these exposures are small. But like a phone running too many apps at once — each one seems minor, but together they drain the battery and slow everything down — these add up across every product you touch throughout the day. The goal isn't eliminating every single one; it's reducing the overall load. Nothing here requires prior knowledge; this is everything you need to follow the rest of the plan.

What are VOCs? Volatile Organic Compounds — chemicals that evaporate easily into the air at room temperature. This is why new paint, new furniture, and synthetic fragrance all have a distinct "smell" — you're breathing the compound itself.
Polyester
Nylon and spandex too — chemically, these are all a type of plastic. Worn directly against skin for hours at a time in clothing, underwear, and sleepwear.
Fragrance
A legal catch-all term; companies aren't required to disclose what's inside it. Absorbed through skin and inhalation.
Phthalates
Often hidden inside "fragrance." Absorbed through skin, inhalation, and internal tissue. Linked to hormone disruption.
BPA
Used in some plastics and can linings. Mimics estrogen in the body. Heat and repeated use increase how much leaches into food or liquid.
PFAS ("forever chemicals")
Used in non-stick coatings and water-resistant fabrics. Doesn't break down in the body or environment over time.
Parabens
Preservatives in personal care products. Absorbed through skin. Some studies link them to hormone disruption.
Lead
Found in some older plumbing and, per recent research, some menstrual products. No safe exposure level established.
Chloramine / Chlorine
Water disinfectants. Absorbed through skin and inhalation, especially in hot showers.

Shower filterHigh impact
Currently: unfiltered shower
Hot water opens pores and lungs to whatever's in the water — chlorine and chloramine absorb faster in the shower than by drinking the same water. This applies just as much to the kids' bath/shower as to yours — worth covering both.
San Diego's own water data: chloramine is the primary disinfectant here, averaging roughly 1–1.7 ppm across the system — and unlike plain chlorine, standard carbon or Brita-style filters don't reliably remove it. San Diego's water is also very hard (16–18 grains per gallon), which compounds skin and hair dryness in the shower specifically. A real, peer-reviewed study measured increased chloroform levels in participants' breath after just a 10-minute shower — confirmed absorption through skin and inhalation, not just theoretical.
Beyond chloramine, San Diego's water has 13 contaminants confirmed above EWG health guidelines by their independent database, including chromium (hexavalent) — the carcinogen made widely known by the Erin Brockovich case — along with several disinfection byproducts (haloacetic acids, trihalomethanes) and uranium. None of this is unique to San Diego or a sign of a poorly-run utility; it reflects how disinfection byproducts form nationwide, and how far EWG's health-based guidelines sit below the legal minimum.

CookwareModerate
Currently: mostly ceramic-coated non-stick, with some stainless already in rotation
The good news: quality ceramic coating is truly free of PFAS and PTFE (the chemicals in traditional Teflon), so this isn't the same category of concern. The real issue is what happens over time — a 2016 peer-reviewed study found titanium dioxide nanoparticles (used in many ceramic coatings) can migrate into food, and once the coating scratches or wears, most ceramic-coated pans expose bare aluminum underneath. Cast iron and stainless have nothing to wear off in the first place.
You already own some stainless pieces — worth reaching for those first, more often, before buying anything new. One scratched ceramic pan got tossed during the visit itself, a real start already made.
